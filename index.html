Surya Pratap Singh Solanki

I combine density functional theory, kinetic modeling and bench-scale reactor experiments to uncover reaction mechanisms and design catalysts for cleaner emissions, better use of methane, and electrochemical CO2 conversion.

I am a chemical engineer working at the intersection of computational modeling and experimental validation. At the University of Houston, I combined density functional theory (DFT) with bench-scale reactor studies to elucidate the fundamental mechanisms governing emission-control catalysts. At Cummins, I translated those insights into predictive kinetic models for full-scale aftertreatment systems. Currently, in Prof. Axel Groß’s group at Universität Ulm, I apply this multiscale approach to the electrochemical conversion of CO2 into hard carbon. Going forward, I aim to lead an independent research program that bridges atomistic mechanisms with catalyst design and macroscopic reactor engineering, advancing cleaner exhaust systems, efficient methane valorization and carbon-neutral chemical processes.

    Research

    I use theory and experiment together to make catalytic processes cleaner and more efficient. My PhD and postdoctoral work so far has centered on three themes.

    Emission Control

    Passive NOx adsorption on Pd-CHA zeolites: a complete mechanism for NOx uptake and oxidation on dynamically hydrated Pd sites, where water and CO shape the active site.

    • DFT benchmarking and vibrational analysis tied to IR fingerprints
    • Bench-scale flow reactor experiments on monolith Pd/zeolite

    Dynamic Catalysis

    Rich–lean feed cycling for methane partial oxidation to syngas on Pt/Al2O3, and surface charge as a lever on CO binding on Pt in a catalytic condenser.

    • Periodic operation to reach non-steady-state surface coverages
    • DFT charge distribution and CO binding energy versus surface charge

    CO2 Electrochemical Conversion

    Cerium-based electrocatalysts that turn CO2 into hard carbon, where oxygen vacancies and the Ce3+/Ce4+ redox couple control nucleation and C–C coupling.

    • DFT mechanism under electrochemical conditions
    • Link between computed active sites and scalable catalyst design

    My vision is a closed loop between prediction and measurement. Descriptor-based screening and data science point to promising catalysts, DFT and ab initio molecular dynamics explain how they work, and reactor experiments with in situ characterization test the predictions. I apply this loop to applications in thermal catalysis (such as methane to syngas and the cooperation of nanoparticles and single atoms) and in electrocatalysis (CO2 conversion to oxalate and Ni-based oxidation catalysts), described below.

    Thermal catalysis

    Cooperative effects of nanoparticles and single atoms on catalytic reactivity

    When CO pulls atoms out of nanoparticles, single atoms and particles coexist and can cooperate.    Details & references

    Supported metal catalysts are not static. Adsorbates such as CO can pull atoms out of nanoparticles and steps, so that single atoms, clusters and particles coexist and the number and type of active sites change with the reaction conditions. On Rh/Al2O3, CO-induced disintegration of nanoparticles into single atoms changes the pre-exponential factor of CO oxidation, which means a turnover frequency is only meaningful once the dynamic site count is known.1 A single descriptor, the adatom formation energy, can screen which metal and adsorbate combinations are prone to this behavior.2 The coexisting sites can also cooperate: a Co single-atom and Co nanocluster catalyst performs a hydrogen-transfer cascade better than either site alone, because each site carries out a different elementary step and hydrogen spills over between them.3

    I want to combine these ideas to ask when nanoparticles and single atoms coexist, which site performs which elementary step, and how the balance shifts with temperature and feed composition. DFT and machine-learned interatomic potentials map the sites and their energetics, and kinetics with in situ infrared spectroscopy test whether the predicted site distribution explains the measured rates. This builds on my work on dynamically changing Pd sites in zeolites and on methane partial oxidation under periodic feed.

    Electrocatalysis

    Material screening for CO2 conversion to oxalate

    Screening metal- and nitrogen-doped graphene for selective CO2 conversion to oxalate.    Details & references

    Converting CO2 to oxalate is attractive for two reasons. Solid oxalates can lock CO2 away for long-term storage, and oxalate is the desired discharge product of lithium-CO2 batteries, which would avoid the poorly reversible carbonate that limits cycling. Graphene doped with nitrogen and single metal atoms (M-Nx-C) is a promising platform, but experiments on nitrogen-doped graphene still show carbonate formation,1 and DFT screening of M-N3-C sites has so far pointed to Ti as the best of the metals studied.2 Machine-learning-guided screening has been applied to Li-CO2 cathodes, but not to graphene-based materials.3

    My approach is to screen defect sites, N-doping patterns and metal centers in graphene with machine-learned potentials, then use DFT with implicit solvation on the most promising candidates to compare oxalate against carbonate and CO formation, and to explain the origin of selectivity through d-band, charge-transfer and CO2 activation analysis. The resulting reaction dataset is intended to be shared openly for the wider CO2 electroconversion community.

    2. Zhou, Y.; Wu, X.; Zhu, P.; Zhang, W. Single-atom catalysts for CO2 reduction to oxalate: theoretical design and reaction condition prediction. ACS Appl. Mater. Interfaces 2025, 17, 49564–49572. doi:10.1021/acsami.5c11771
    3. Yao, T.; et al. High-throughput computation and machine learning prediction accelerating the design of cathode catalysts for Li-CO2 batteries. J. Phys. Chem. C 2024, 128, 11534–11542. doi:10.1021/acs.jpcc.4c02641
    Electrocatalysis

    Ni-based catalysts for oxidation reactions

    First-principles models of how Ni oxide surfaces respond to the electrochemical environment.    Details & references

    Ni (oxy)hydroxides are among the most cost-effective anodes for the oxygen evolution reaction in alkaline electrolysis, and are also used for the oxidation of organic molecules such as alcohols. Yet the structure of the active surface under operating conditions is still debated. Grand-canonical DFT shows that deprotonation and alkali cation adsorption at the NiOOH(0001)/water interface depend strongly on the surface dipole and on explicit water.1 Surface Pourbaix diagrams show that interlayer hydrogen bonding in NiOOH governs surface redox behavior and reactivity.2 Most recently, isotope-labelled surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy combined with computed Raman spectra has been used to argue that the surface is terminated by NiO2 rather than NiOOH during oxygen evolution, with bands previously assigned to superoxides attributed to overtones and a vibrational Stark effect.3

    I am working on first-principles models of how the Ni oxide surface responds to the electrochemical environment. The aim is to connect the computed surface structures and oxidation states to spectroscopic signatures and to the reactivity of the oxidation reaction.

    2. Sun, W.; Govindarajan, N.; Prajapati, A.; Huang, J.; Bemana, H.; Feaster, J. T.; Akhade, S. A.; Kornienko, N.; Hahn, C. Insights into the electrochemical oxidation and reduction of nickel oxide surfaces. ACS Appl. Mater. Interfaces 2025, 17, 2365–2375. doi:10.1021/acsami.4c13187

    Teaching Interests

    Alongside a rigorous research program, I am deeply committed to undergraduate and graduate education in chemical engineering. My teaching rests on three principles.

    Student-Centered Learning

    Fostering instructor–student rapport, involving students in course design, and using cooperative learning to build collaborative skills.

    Bridging Theory and Practice

    Case studies, hands-on projects and industry-relevant examples that connect fundamentals with real-world application.

    Continuous Improvement

    Seeking student feedback through surveys, office hours and post-exam reflections to adapt teaching to evolving needs.

    Courses

    Undergraduate: I am prepared to teach chemical reaction engineering, mass transfer and heat transfer.

    Graduate: I aspire to develop an advanced course in reaction engineering and to design a course on computational methods in catalysis that integrates DFT, kinetic modeling and data-driven workflows.

    Experience: Teaching assistant for Analytical Methods for Chemical Engineers (CHEE 3321) and Bio & Physical Chemistry (CHEE 3466) at the University of Houston, which strengthened my commitment to transformative, inclusive instruction.
